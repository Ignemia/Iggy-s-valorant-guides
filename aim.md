# Theory of aim
> Many coaches will try to steer clear of aim as a part of the game what so ever. Others, less experienced coaches will, on the other hand, put too much value into aim. I stand somewhere in the middle. At the end of the day, we are talking about a shooter game. When push comnes to shove, you have to hit your shots. That's why I want to talk about aim. So that when that happens you actually hit the shots.
> **In this article I will try to talk about 3 main points:**
> 1. How does aim work from physiological perspective
> 2. What are the components of aim in game
> 3. How to practice and improve aim

## How do we aim from physiological perspective?
> **!! Disclaimer !! I do not hold a degree in medicine or medical science, nor neurology or any other affiliated field. All that I say here is from personal observations and reflection of my experience**

From what I have observed aim happens in total of 5 stages, of which one can be fully elimintated by preparation:
1. Target recognition
2. Flick
3. Flick error recognition
4. Micro adjustment
5. Trigger

So let's break these stages down so we fully understand what happens in each one:
### Target recognition
> From observing low elo players, this is the one many struggle with a lot. While almost everyone sometimes misses an opponent on the screen, in lower elo it is almost a round-by-round occurance.

An opponent enters your FOV (field of view) and you can clearly see them on your screen. Your brain has to process what it is seeing. Valorant adds colored outlines to opponents (red, purple or yellow) to make this step easier. The time it takes your brain to recognize "Oh that purple outlined blob in the bottom left corner of the screen is an opponent" is usually what I like to call "pre-reaction time", in the hollistic picture it is part of reaction time but I find it better to categorize it as separate timing for future reference.  
During this stage a lot of things have to happen:
- Your brain has to register change in colors in a specific area of the screen
- Categorize those colors as "belonging to the opposing team"
- Often categorize what opponent it is (agent)
- Categorize threat level

**This is a lot to do in matter of milliseconds, so this becomes the longest or second longest time of the whole aim process**

***This part of the process usually takes between 50-150ms.***

### Flick
> The easiest part to practice, the most error prone mid-round, we eliminate it by proper crosshair placements and pre-fire

This part is very self explanatory.  
Your brain has recognized target, your crosshair is nowhere near their head or the target in general.  
Now brain calculates how much to move the corsshair to be on target and sends that information over neural pathways towards the forearm. And yes it is very important. **This part is performed by foreaim to prevent strain or injury of the wrist.**  
Now muscle memory of the forearm muscles kicks in and the forearm moves as much as it assumes is correct for the amount of on-screen movement that is needed.  

*Because we are relying on muscle memory not a true brain calculation this part is very imprecise and causes the need for next 2 steps after each flick even if you land straight on, the brain has over time learnt to check the work of the muscle and it will go through the next step or, in case if error is detected, the next 2 steps anyway.*  

***The brain calculating offset and sending the signal over neural pathway to forearm usually takes between 10-25ms (with the arrival of the signal to forearm which triggers the first movement in direction to target we finish reaction time and start counting "time to kill"). The movement itself can take between 150ms to 2 seconds (depending on training, quality of muscle memory and distance to travel)***

### Flick error detection
When the flick is done it's movement you will, most of the time, be near the target but exactly on. This difference between where you should've landed and where you landed is what I call "flick error".  
This step happens in brain and is very quick because you already know about the target, you know all the relevant information about the target, now all that matters is how much did you miss them by.  

***This step usually takes between 10-25ms***

### Micro-adjustment (or just adjustment)
> This is the part you practice most in range when you do that classic "pick two bots and switch between them with a classic" practice.  

When the flick error is detected and calculated the brain send this information to the wrist. And once more this is important. This part is done by the wrist because you have finer motor control in the wrist and you need to make smaller movements.
Muscle memory of the wrist takes over and places the crosshair with very good accuracy on the head of the target or on the target in general.  

**This is the most consequential step of the aim process, if we overlook target detection which starts the whole thing.**  
***This step usually takes between 50-500ms from flick error detection to finishing the movement of the wrist. Once against depending on quality of muscle memory and distance to cover.***

### Trigger
> This is the only part that can almost never be detected by anti-cheats and that's why so many trigger-bot cheats exist.

You are on target. You don't need to adjust the aim anymore.
Your brain has recognized none or close to none error or distance to target. It sends the signal to the finger (usually index finger) to press down on the mouse button (for right-hand laterity it is the left mouse button most often) and shoot.
This is very fast and given all that precedes it, it should be.

***This step usually takes between 10-30ms. Could take longer if your mouse button is stiff.***
